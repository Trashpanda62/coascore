Purity is not quantity

Net peptide content: why a 10 mg vial is not 10 mg of peptide

HPLC purity says how much of the detected material is the target peptide. It does not say how much of the powder in the vial is peptide at all.

Research Use Only. This guide explains analytical documentation, not use in humans.

Two questions, two numbers

A peptide COA usually reports HPLC purity. That number describes the sample's composition as the detector sees it: of everything that produced a peak, how much was the target peptide. It does not say how much of the vial's contents is peptide. That is net peptide content, and it is a separate measurement.

What else is in a freeze-dried vial

A lyophilized peptide is rarely 100% peptide by mass. The powder can also hold:

  • Bound water. Freeze-dried peptides take up moisture.
  • Counterions. Synthesis and purification leave the peptide as a salt, commonly acetate or trifluoroacetate (TFA). The counterion has mass but may not show up as a peak under a peptide HPLC method.
  • Residual salts and solvents from manufacturing.
  • Added excipients such as a bulking agent, if the product has one.

None of these lower the HPLC area-%. All of them lower the share of the powder that is peptide.

Net peptide content, defined

Net peptide content is the mass of the peptide itself divided by the total mass of the sample, expressed as a percentage. If the report gives it, it should also say which salt form it refers to and what it was measured against.

A worked example (made-up numbers)

Suppose a vial is labeled 10 mg and a report shows a net peptide content of 80%. The vial then holds about 8 mg of peptide, not 10 mg. If you dissolve it in 2 mL, the solution is about 4 mg/mL. Assume the full 10 mg and you would calculate 5 mg/mL, a 25% overstatement. The HPLC purity on the same report could be 99% and the arithmetic would not change, because purity says nothing about the other 2 mg.

These figures are an illustration of the math. They are not a typical or expected value for any product. Check the reconstitution calculator with your own inputs.

How content gets measured

Quantity needs a method built to measure quantity. Depending on the lab's validated procedure, that can be:

  • amino acid analysis;
  • quantitative NMR (qNMR);
  • an HPLC assay calibrated against a characterized reference standard;
  • another suitable quantitative method the report describes.

An uncalibrated HPLC area percentage is not on this list.

What to look for on a COA

  1. A separate content line. It should be distinct from the purity line, with its own method name.
  2. Units and basis. Percent of what: total powder mass, or peptide on an anhydrous or salt-free basis?
  3. The salt form. Acetate, TFA, or free base changes the molecular weight used in the math. The peptide reference notes the salt form behind each listed mass.
  4. Calibration detail. Reference standard, preparation, and any stated uncertainty or acceptance range.
  5. The same sample. Lot and report identifiers should match the vial in front of you.

If the COA has no content measurement, treat the labeled vial amount as the manufacturer's claim rather than a documented result. The COA Interpreter has optional fields for net peptide content, salt form, and label strength, and flags when they are missing.
