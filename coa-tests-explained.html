What a COA can and cannot show

Which tests belong on a peptide COA, and what each one proves

A COA is only as broad as the tests listed on it. Each test answers one question. This guide matches the question to the test, and says what the report should show before you accept the answer.

Research Use Only. This guide explains analytical documentation, not use in humans.

Short answer. Most research-peptide COAs show two tests: HPLC (purity) and mass spectrometry (identity). Amount, water, counterions, residual solvents, metals, sterility and endotoxin are separate tests. If the report does not list one, it has not shown it.

Test by test

Read across a row: the question, the test that answers it, what the report should show, and what the result still leaves open.

QuestionTestA report should showIt does not tell you
Is it the right molecule?Mass spectrometry (ESI-MS, MALDI-TOF, or LC-MS)Observed m/z peaks, the charge states used, the expected mass it was compared against, and the instrument or method.Purity or amount. Different molecules can share a mass, so a match supports identity and does not prove it.
Is the sequence right?Amino acid analysis, or MS/MS sequencingAmino acid analysis gives the ratio of residues. MS/MS gives fragment ions and how much of the sequence they cover.Amino acid analysis gives composition, not order. Neither is routine on low-cost COAs.
How pure is it?Reversed-phase HPLC (or UPLC) with UV detectionColumn, gradient, detection wavelength, the full chromatogram and a peak table with the integration.Amount in the vial. Purity is relative peak area. Impurities that co-elute or do not absorb at the detection wavelength are invisible.
How much peptide is in the powder?Net peptide content (amino acid analysis, nitrogen analysis, or quantitative NMR)A percentage by mass, the salt form it refers to, and the method.Purity. A sample can be 99% pure by HPLC and still be well under 100% peptide by mass. See net peptide content.
How much water is in it?Karl Fischer titration, or loss on dryingPercent water by mass, and the method.Identity or purity. It is one part of why powder mass is not peptide mass.
Which counterion, and how much?Ion chromatography for acetate or chloride; ion chromatography or 19F NMR for trifluoroacetate (TFA)The counterion, its percent by mass, and the method.Whether the peptide itself is correct. Counterion mass is another reason labeled amount and peptide amount differ.
Are there leftover solvents?Headspace gas chromatographyEach solvent tested, the result in ppm or percent, and the limit it was compared with. ICH Q3C is the usual reference for limits.Anything about the peptide. It is rare on research COAs.
Are there metals?ICP-MS or ICP-OES (elemental impurities)The elements tested, the result per element with units, and the limit. ICH Q3D and USP <232> are the usual references.Anything about the peptide. A pass/fail line with no element list shows little.
Is it free of microbes?Sterility test (USP <71>), or a bioburden count (USP <61>)Method, sample size, incubation time, the pass/fail result and the date. A sterility test is run over about 14 days.Endotoxin. A sterile sample can still carry endotoxin from dead bacteria.
Is endotoxin low?Bacterial endotoxins test (LAL; USP <85>), gel-clot or kineticA numeric result in endotoxin units (EU) per mg or per mL, the limit, the method and the lot tested.Sterility. A bare “endotoxin: pass” with no number and no limit cannot be checked.

The two tests you will see most

HPLC and mass spectrometry come first because they are the cheapest way to check purity and identity together. Our guide to reading a chromatogram and mass spectrum shows what to look for in each. For the expected mass of a specific compound, use the peptide reference.

If the seller says it, ask for the report

  • “Sterile.” Ask for a sterility test report: method, sample size, incubation time, result, date and lot. A sentence in a product description is not a test.
  • “Endotoxin tested.” Ask for the number in EU, the limit and the method, for the lot you hold.
  • “99% pure.” Ask how it was measured. If it is an HPLC area percentage, it says nothing about how much peptide is in the vial.
  • “Heavy metals tested.” Ask which elements, with each result and unit.
  • “Third-party tested.” See how to verify a COA with the lab itself.

Numbers beat “pass”

A result of “pass” is only as good as the limit behind it. A report that gives a number, the unit and the limit lets you check the claim. A report that gives only “pass” asks you to trust it. For the same reason, one lot’s report does not cover another lot. Check that the lot number on the report matches the vial.

What a missing test means

A missing test is a gap in the evidence, not proof of a problem. Many research COAs are short on purpose. Treat the gaps as questions you can put to the seller or the lab. Our printable checklist has a line for each, and the COA Interpreter reads a report line by line.

Questions to put to the lab

  1. Which tests were run on this exact lot, and which were not?
  2. What method and instrument did each use?
  3. For each result, what is the limit or specification?
  4. What does the purity number refer to: peak area, or mass?
  5. Can the lab confirm the report number and date?

Common questions

What tests should a peptide COA include?

Most research-peptide COAs show two: HPLC for purity and mass spectrometry for identity. Quantity (net peptide content), water, counterions, residual solvents, metals, sterility and endotoxin are separate tests. A report covers only the tests it actually lists, with method and result.

Is HPLC purity the same as peptide content?

No. HPLC purity is the share of detected peak area that is the target peptide. Net peptide content is the share of the powder’s mass that is peptide. Water and counterions lower the second number and not the first.

Does a peptide COA show endotoxin or sterility results?

Only if those tests were run and listed. A report that says nothing about them has not shown them. If a seller says a product is sterile or endotoxin tested, ask for the report with the method, the numeric result and the limit.

What does a mass spectrometry result on a COA prove?

It shows the sample contains something with the expected mass, which supports identity. It does not show purity or amount, and different molecules can share a mass.

Where these tests are defined

Named chapters, for those who want the source: USP <61> microbial enumeration, USP <71> sterility tests, USP <85> bacterial endotoxins, USP <232> elemental impurities, and the ICH guidelines Q3C (residual solvents) and Q3D (elemental impurities), available from ich.org. Pharmacopoeia chapters are published behind a subscription. We name them so you can ask a lab which one it followed, not to suggest that a research COA must follow them.
