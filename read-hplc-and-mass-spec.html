The two pictures on a COA

How to read the chromatogram and mass spectrum on a peptide COA

Most peptide COAs include two plots. One speaks to relative composition, the other to identity. Here is what each can and cannot show.

Research Use Only. This guide explains analytical documentation, not use in humans.

The chromatogram: what the picture shows

An HPLC chromatogram plots detector signal (the vertical axis) against retention time (the horizontal axis). Each peak is a group of molecules that left the column at the same time. For peptides the detector is usually UV absorbance at a short wavelength such as 214 or 220 nm, where the peptide bond absorbs.

  • The main peak should be the target peptide. Its retention time alone does not prove that, because retention time depends on the method.
  • Smaller peaks are other detected species: synthesis by-products, truncated or modified peptides, or degradation products.
  • Peaks very early in the run often come from the solvent front or injection, not from the sample. A good report states what was excluded from the total.
  • The baseline should be flat or smoothly shifting with the gradient. A noisy or drifting baseline makes small peaks hard to separate from noise.

Integration decides the percentage

Area-% is only as sound as the integration behind it. The software draws a baseline and sets where each peak starts and ends. Those choices can move the result by a fraction of a percent or more, especially when a small impurity sits on the shoulder of the main peak. Look for:

  1. A full-length trace from injection to the end of the run, not a cropped window around the main peak.
  2. A peak table with retention times and areas that add up to the stated percentage.
  3. Visible integration marks showing where each peak was cut.
  4. Method conditions: column, mobile phases, gradient, flow rate, wavelength.
  5. The same sample ID on the trace as on the summary page.

The mass spectrum: what the picture shows

A mass spectrometer reports mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), not mass directly. Peptides usually pick up several protons, so one peptide appears as a series of peaks, one for each charge state n. For a neutral mass M the expected peak is at (M + n × 1.00728) / n.

Worked example using semaglutide's published molecular weight of about 4,113.6 Da:

  • 4+ charge state: m/z about 1,029.4
  • 5+ charge state: m/z about 823.7
  • 6+ charge state: m/z about 686.6

So a spectrum with strong peaks near those values supports a mass near 4,113.6 Da. Many reports also show a deconvoluted spectrum that converts the series back to a single neutral mass. Either view lets you compare the observed mass with the expected one.

Things to check in the mass result

  • Observed versus theoretical mass, and the tolerance the lab applies. Both numbers should appear.
  • Average or monoisotopic mass. For a peptide this size the two differ by a few daltons, so make sure you are comparing like with like.
  • The sample ID and instrument are named on the spectrum.

What neither picture tells you

A clean chromatogram describes relative composition. A matching mass supports identity. Neither one measures how much peptide is in the vial. That needs a quantitative method; see net peptide content.
