Proteins, glycoproteins and mixtures

Why some peptides have no single molecular weight

A small peptide is one exact molecule, so a COA can state its mass. Some products sold as “peptides” are not that. They are large proteins, sugar-coated proteins, or mixtures. This guide covers four of them and says what a COA should show instead.

Research use only. This explains analytical documentation. It is not dosing, medical or purchasing advice.

Short answer. HCG and follistatin carry sugar chains that vary, thymalin is a mixture, and IGF-1 LR3 is a protein whose quoted mass differs by source. For these, identity comes from potency, immunoassay, SDS-PAGE or an intact-protein mass spectrum, not from one tidy molecular weight. Treat a single exact mass printed to two decimals with caution.

Why sugar chains and mixtures break the single-mass rule

A mass spectrometer weighs individual molecules. A small synthetic peptide gives one clean mass because every molecule is identical. A glycoprotein is made by cells, and each molecule can carry a slightly different set of sugar chains. The sample is a family of masses, so the instrument shows a spread, and gel results (SDS-PAGE) show a band or smear at an apparent size that need not equal the calculated one. A mixture such as thymalin is worse: there is no single target to weigh at all.

What a COA should show instead

  1. Identity. The method named and the data attached: an immunoassay or activity result for HCG and follistatin, an intact-protein spectrum for IGF-1 LR3. A statement of “identity confirmed” alone is not data.
  2. Purity by a fitting method. SDS-PAGE or RP-HPLC for proteins, with the image or trace. A small-peptide HPLC percentage does not describe a glycoprotein.
  3. Potency or quantity. HCG is measured in international units against a reference, so the report should state the unit and the reference used. For any protein, ask how the amount was measured.
  4. Safety tests where relevant. Endotoxin and sterility matter more for recombinant products. See which tests belong on a COA.
  5. Traceability. Lot, sample ID, lab, dates, report number, as for any COA. See how to verify a COA.

Red flags specific to these products

  • One exact molecular weight, to two decimals, for HCG, follistatin or thymalin.
  • A bare “99% HPLC” with no identity or potency data for a glycoprotein.
  • A mass that matches a different compound, such as a dipeptide named in place of a thymus extract. Check the name against the reference.
  • No isoform stated for follistatin (for example FS-315 or FS-288).
  • A quoted mass with no method, or a vendor and a lab that quote different values for the same lot.

Questions to put to the lab or seller

  1. What exactly was tested, and against what reference?
  2. How was identity shown, and can I see the raw data?
  3. Which form or isoform does this report cover?
  4. How was the amount measured, and in what unit?
  5. Which lot and report number can I confirm with the lab directly?

Common questions

Why does my HCG COA not list a molecular weight?

HCG is a glycoprotein made of two chains carrying sugar chains of varying size. The product is a population of slightly different molecules, so identity is normally shown by potency in IU or by an immunoassay, not by one mass. If a report prints one exact molecular weight for it, ask where the number came from.

Is a mass-spec result useful for a protein like IGF-1 LR3?

Yes, if the report shows the spectrum and says how the mass was derived. Large proteins carry many charges, so labs usually convert the peaks to one mass. Compare that mass with about 9.1 kDa and ask for the spectrum, not only a number.

Does 99% HPLC purity prove a protein is what the label says?

No. Purity says how much of the detected signal is one peak. It does not say that the peak is the named protein, how active it is, or how much is in the vial. Ask for an identity test and a quantity method as well.

Why do two vendors quote different molecular weights for the same product?

They may be quoting different things: a calculated mass from the sequence, a measured mass of one form, an average over glycoforms, or a different isoform. A quoted mass without its source and method is weak evidence.
